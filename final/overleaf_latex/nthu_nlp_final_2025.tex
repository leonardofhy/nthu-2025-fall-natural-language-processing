\begin{abstract}
  In this work, we present a robust solution for the Google QUEST Q\&A Labeling challenge, which aims to estimate subjective quality scores for question-answer pairs. Our approach significantly improves upon a strong baseline (23rd place solution) by systematically addressing its limitations. We transition from a BERT-based architecture to DeBERTa-v3-Large to leverage its disentangled attention mechanism, and we identify that increasing the sequence length to 1024 tokens is critical for capturing the full context of Q\&A pairs. Furthermore, we address the label noise inherent in subjective ratings by replacing the standard Binary Cross Entropy (BCE) loss with a ranking-aware objective combined with a differentiable Soft Spearman loss. Finally, we adopt a two-phase training strategy to stabilize the learning of question-specific context. Our final model achieves a Cross-Validation (CV) Spearman correlation of 0.4228, outperforming the baseline's 0.4195, and demonstrates the importance of ranking-oriented objectives in subjective quality estimation tasks.
\end{abstract}


\section{Task Description}

The Google QUEST Q\&A Labeling competition challenges participants to improved automated methods for estimating the quality of question-answer pairs from the StackExchange dataset. The goal is to predict human-generated quality scores for various subjective aspects of Q\&A pairs.

\subsection{Problem Definition}
This is a multi-label regression task. For each sample, the input consists of a question title, a question body, and an answer. The output is a set of 30 scalar values in the range $[0, 1]$, representing 30 distinct quality labels. These labels cover two main categories:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Question Ratings}: 21 labels evaluating the question's intent, type (e.g., instructional, conversational), and quality (e.g., well-written, helpful).
    \item \textbf{Answer Ratings}: 9 labels evaluating the answer's quality (e.g., helpfulness, plausibility, detailedness).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Evaluation Metric}
The performance is evaluated using the \textbf{Spearman's rank correlation coefficient}. The Spearman correlation is calculated for each of the 30 target columns between the predicted and ground truth values. The final score is the mean of these 30 column-wise correlations. This metric emphasizes the relative ranking of predictions rather than their absolute values, which poses a unique challenge for standard regression loss functions like Mean Squared Error (MSE) or Binary Cross Entropy (BCE).

\subsection{Key Challenges}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Subjectivity}: The labels are derived from human raters and often represent subjective opinions rather than objective truths, introducing label noise.
    \item \textbf{Data Scarcity}: The dataset contains approximately 6,000 training samples and fewer than 500 test samples, creating a high risk of overfitting.
    \item \textbf{Correlation Sensitivity}: The Spearman metric requires the model to correctly rank samples, making it sensitive to distribution shifts and requiring ranking-aware optimization.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Works}

\subsection{Baseline Overview}
We select the 23rd place solution from the Kaggle competition as our primary baseline. This solution employs a \texttt{bert-base-uncased} architecture with a maximum sequence length of 512 tokens. A key feature of this baseline is its use of a custom pooling strategy, which concatenates the \texttt{[CLS]} and \texttt{[SEP]} token representations from the last four hidden layers handled by separate heads. The model is trained using a \textbf{Ranking + Binary Cross Entropy (BCE)} loss function, which combines pairwise ranking with soft classification. To mitigate overfitting on the small dataset, the baseline adopts a "Two-Phase Training" strategy: first training on the question title and body only (Question-Only phase) to learn robust question representations, followed by fine-tuning on the full Q\&A pairs. Post-processing involves LightGBM stacking to refine predictions.

\section{Methods}
We propose several architectural and methodological improvements to address the limitations of the baseline.

\subsection{Model Architecture}
We replace the BERT backbone with \texttt{microsoft/deberta-v3-large}. DeBERTa (Decoding-enhanced BERT with disentangled attention) improves upon BERT and RoBERTa by using a disentangled attention mechanism and an enhanced mask decoder. This is particularly beneficial for our task, which involves determining the quality of answers relative to questions, often requiring the model to capture long-range dependencies and subtle relative positions of tokens.

\subsection{Pooling Strategy}
Unlike the baseline's custom pooling, we adopt a \textbf{Weighted Layer Pooling} followed by \textbf{Attention Pooling}. We extract the hidden states from the last four layers of the encoder and compute a weighted average, where the weights are learnable parameters. These weighted embeddings are then passed through an Attention Pooling layer, which learns to emphasize the most informative tokens in the sequence before generating the final fixed-size representation for classification.

\subsection{Input Optimization}
We identified that the baseline's 512-token limit was a significant bottleneck, causing critical information to be truncated. We increase the maximum sequence length to \textbf{1024 tokens}. This ensures that the model can attend to the entire question body and answer text simultaneously, which is crucial for evaluating metrics like "detailedness" or "relevance".

\subsection{Optimization Objectives}
The evaluation metric is Spearman's rank correlation, which relies on the relative ordering of predictions rather than their absolute values. The baseline uses a Ranking + BCE loss. While the ranking component is beneficial, BCE penalizes absolute error, which can be suboptimal for noisy subjective labels. We keep the Ranking Loss but replace BCE with a \textbf{Soft Spearman Loss}---a differentiable approximation of the Spearman correlation, enabling direct optimization of the evaluation metric.

The final loss is a weighted combination of these ranking-aware objectives:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} = \lambda_{\text{rank}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{ranking}} + \lambda_{\text{spearman}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{soft-spearman}}
\end{equation}


\subsection{Training Strategy}
We adopt the baseline's effective \textbf{Two-Phase Training} strategy. In Phase 1, we train the model for 5 epochs on Question-Only data to stabilize the encoder's understanding of the question context. In Phase 2, we train for another 5 epochs on the full Question-Answer pairs.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
We perform 5-fold cross-validation on the training set. The model is optimized using the AdamW optimizer with a layer-wise learning rate decay. We evaluate performance using the mean Spearman correlation coefficient across all 30 target columns.

\subsection{Results}
Table \ref{tab:results} summarizes our experimental results. We start with the BERT-base baseline (Baseline 1). Simply switching to DeBERTa-base with our new pooling strategy but keeping the 512 context length (Baseline 2) resulted in a performance drop ("The Dip"), likely due to the model being larger but "starved" of context or the change in pooling logic not being effective without sufficient information.

Increasing the sequence length to 1024 (Step 1) provided an immediate recovery and boost (+0.0155), confirming that context is the primary bottleneck. The introduction of the Ranking and Soft Spearman losses (Step 2) yielded the largest single improvement (+0.0229), proving that aligning the loss function with the metric is critical. Finally, scaling to the Large model and using the full training schedule (Step 3 \& Final) achieved our best single-model performance of \textbf{0.4228}, surpassing the baseline's 0.4195.

\subsection{Discussion}
\subsubsection{The CV vs LB Paradox}
Observing Table \ref{tab:results}, we note a paradox: while our final single-model CV score (\textbf{0.4228}) surpasses the baseline's CV (0.4195), our Private Leaderboard (LB) score (0.4066) is lower than the baseline's (0.4144).

\textbf{Explanation: The 2-Hour Inference Limit.} The competition imposes a strict 2-hour limit on inference. The baseline uses the lightweight \texttt{bert-base-uncased} architecture (110M parameters). This computational efficiency allowed the baseline solution to employ \textbf{Snapshot Ensembling}, averaging predictions from over 30 model checkpoints within the time limit. Ensembling is known to significantly boost generalization and stability, directly improving the LB score.

In contrast, our solution uses \texttt{deberta-v3-large} (435M parameters), which is significantly more computationally expensive. Due to the 2-hour constraint, we were limited to identifying and submitting a \textbf{single best model}. While our single model demonstrates superior quality per parameter (evidenced by the higher CV), it lacks the variance-reduction benefits of an ensemble.

\textbf{Conclusion:} We successfully improved the \textbf{model quality} through architectural and objective optimizations, but the baseline maintained an advantage in \textbf{ensemble capacity} due to the specific inference time constraints of this competition.

\section{Conclusion}
In this report, we demonstrated that modernizing the backbone architecture to DeBERTa-v3 and, crucially, aligning the training objectives with the ranking nature of the task are key to improving performance on the Google QUEST Q\&A Labeling challenge. We showed that simple MSE/BCE losses are insufficient for subjective scoring tasks and that a Ranking + Soft Spearman loss significantly boosts correlation. Additionally, we highlighted the importance of sufficient context length (1024 tokens) for Transformer-based models to effective process long Q\&A pairs. Future work could explore advanced post-processing techniques such as distribution matching or stacking to further bridge the gap between CV and Private Leaderboard scores.
