\begin{abstract}
  In this work, we present a robust solution for the Google QUEST Q\&A Labeling challenge, developed as the \textbf{final term project} for the Natural Language Processing course at National Tsing Hua University. Our approach significantly improves upon a strong baseline (23rd place solution) by systematically addressing its limitations. We transition from a BERT-based architecture to DeBERTa-v3-Large to leverage its disentangled attention mechanism, and we identify that increasing the sequence length to 1024 tokens is critical for capturing the full context of Q\&A pairs. Furthermore, we address the label noise inherent in subjective ratings by replacing the standard Binary Cross Entropy (BCE) loss with a ranking-aware objective combined with a differentiable Soft Spearman loss. Finally, we adopt a two-phase training strategy to stabilize the learning of question-specific context. Our final model achieves a Cross-Validation (CV) Spearman correlation of 0.4228, outperforming the baseline's 0.4195, and demonstrates the importance of ranking-oriented objectives in subjective quality estimation tasks.
\end{abstract}


\section{Task Description}

The Google QUEST Q\&A Labeling competition challenges participants to improve automated methods for estimating the quality of question-answer pairs from the StackExchange dataset. The goal is to predict human-generated quality scores for various subjective aspects of Q\&A pairs.

\subsection{Problem Definition}
This is a multi-label regression task. For each sample, the input consists of a question title, a question body, and an answer. The output is a set of 30 scalar values in the range $[0, 1]$, representing 30 distinct quality labels. These labels cover two main categories:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Question Ratings}: 21 labels evaluating the question's intent, type (e.g., instructional, conversational), and quality (e.g., well-written, helpful).
    \item \textbf{Answer Ratings}: 9 labels evaluating the answer's quality (e.g., helpfulness, plausibility, detailedness).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Evaluation Metric}
The performance is evaluated using the \textbf{Spearman's rank correlation coefficient}. The Spearman correlation is calculated for each of the 30 target columns between the predicted and ground truth values. The final score is the mean of these 30 column-wise correlations. This metric emphasizes the relative ranking of predictions rather than their absolute values, which poses a unique challenge for standard regression loss functions like Mean Squared Error (MSE) or Binary Cross Entropy (BCE).

\subsection{Key Challenges}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Subjectivity}: The labels are derived from human raters and often represent subjective opinions rather than objective truths, introducing label noise.
    \item \textbf{Data Scarcity}: The dataset contains approximately 6,000 training samples and fewer than 500 test samples, creating a high risk of overfitting.
    \item \textbf{Correlation Sensitivity}: The Spearman metric requires the model to correctly rank samples, making it sensitive to distribution shifts and requiring ranking-aware optimization.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Baseline Overview}
We select the 23rd place solution from the Kaggle competition as our primary baseline. This solution employs a \texttt{bert-base-uncased} architecture with a maximum sequence length of 512 tokens. A key feature is its custom pooling strategy, which concatenates the \texttt{[CLS]} and \texttt{[SEP]} token representations from the last four hidden layers.

The model is trained using a \textbf{Ranking + Binary Cross Entropy (BCE)} loss function. To mitigate overfitting on the small dataset, the baseline adopts a ``Two-Phase Training'' strategy: first training on question title and body only to learn robust question representations, followed by fine-tuning on full Q\&A pairs. Post-processing involves LightGBM stacking.

\section{Methods}
We introduce four key improvements over the baseline: (1) a stronger backbone, (2) improved pooling, (3) longer context, and (4) ranking-aware optimization.

\subsection{Model Architecture}
We replace the BERT backbone with \texttt{microsoft/deberta-v3-large} [1]. DeBERTa uses a disentangled attention mechanism that separates content and position information, which is beneficial for capturing the relationship between questions and answers across long contexts.

\subsection{Pooling Strategy}
Unlike the baseline's custom pooling, we adopt a \textbf{Weighted Layer Pooling} followed by \textbf{Attention Pooling}. We extract the hidden states from the last four layers of the encoder and compute a weighted average, where the weights are learnable parameters. These weighted embeddings are then passed through an Attention Pooling layer, which learns to emphasize the most informative tokens in the sequence before generating the final fixed-size representation for classification.

\subsection{Context Length Extension}
The baseline's 512-token limit was a significant bottleneck, causing critical information to be truncated. We increase the maximum sequence length to \textbf{1024 tokens}, ensuring that the model can attend to the entire question body and answer text simultaneously. This is crucial for evaluating metrics like ``detailedness'' or ``relevance.''

\subsection{Optimization Objectives}
The evaluation metric is Spearman's rank correlation, which relies on the relative ordering of predictions rather than their absolute values. The baseline uses a Ranking + BCE loss. While the ranking component is beneficial, BCE penalizes absolute error, which can be suboptimal for noisy subjective labels. We keep the Ranking Loss but replace BCE with a \textbf{Soft Spearman Loss}---a differentiable approximation of the Spearman correlation inspired by [5], enabling direct optimization of the evaluation metric.

The Soft Spearman Loss approximates the discrete ranking operation using a sigmoid-based soft rank function:
\begin{equation}
    r_i^{\text{soft}} = \sum_{j} \sigma\left(\frac{x_i - x_j}{\tau}\right)
\end{equation}
where $\tau$ is a temperature parameter controlling the sharpness of the approximation. The final loss is:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{soft-spearman}} = 1 - \rho(r^{\text{soft}}_{\text{pred}}, r^{\text{soft}}_{\text{target}})
\end{equation}
where $\rho$ denotes Pearson correlation applied to the soft ranks.

The final loss is a weighted combination of ranking-aware objectives:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} = \lambda_{\text{rank}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{ranking}} + \lambda_{\text{spearman}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{soft-spearman}}
\end{equation}


\subsection{Training Strategy}
We adopt the baseline's effective \textbf{Two-Phase Training} strategy. In Phase 1, we train the model for 5 epochs on Question-Only data to stabilize the encoder's understanding of the question context. In Phase 2, we train for another 5 epochs on the full Question-Answer pairs.

Table \ref{tab:results} summarizes our experimental results, including both Cross-Validation (CV) scores and Kaggle Leaderboard (LB) scores where available. We start with the BERT-base baseline. Our initial attempt (Exp 0) naively switched to DeBERTa-base with mean pooling while keeping the 512 context length, resulting in a significant performance drop (``The Dip''), which we analyze in detail below.

Increasing the sequence length to 1024 (Exp 1) provided an immediate recovery and boost (+0.0155), confirming that context is the primary bottleneck. The introduction of the Ranking and Soft Spearman losses (Exp 2) yielded the largest single improvement (+0.0229), proving that aligning the loss function with the metric is critical. Finally, scaling to the Large model and using the full training schedule (Exp 3 \& Final) achieved our best single-model performance of \textbf{0.4228}, surpassing the baseline's CV of 0.4195.

\paragraph{Understanding ``The Dip.''}
The severe performance drop when naively switching from BERT to DeBERTa (0.4195 $\rightarrow$ 0.3642) warrants explanation. We identify two compounding factors:

\textbf{Pooling Mismatch.} The baseline explicitly extracted \texttt{[CLS]} and \texttt{[SEP]} token features, which encodes the structural boundary between the question and answer segments. Our naive DeBERTa implementation used mean pooling across all tokens, effectively blending the question and answer representations into a structure-less mixture, losing this critical boundary information.

\textbf{Context Starvation.} DeBERTa's disentangled attention mechanism relies heavily on relative position information across the full sequence context. Truncating inputs to 512 tokens breaks long-range dependencies that the model was designed to exploit, hurting DeBERTa more severely than BERT's absolute positional encoding.

\textbf{Conclusion:} A superior architecture cannot overcome information loss from truncation and structural blindness from inappropriate pooling. This insight directly motivated our switch to longer context (1024 tokens) and structured attention pooling.


\section{Discussion}

\subsection{The CV vs. LB Paradox}
Observing Table \ref{tab:results}, we note a paradox: while our final single-model CV score (\textbf{0.4228}) surpasses the baseline's CV (0.4195), our Private Leaderboard (LB) score (0.4066) is lower than the baseline's (0.4123).

The competition imposes a strict 2-hour limit on inference. The baseline uses the lightweight \texttt{bert-base-uncased} architecture (110M parameters), which allowed it to employ \textbf{Snapshot Ensembling}---averaging predictions from over 30 model checkpoints within the time limit. Ensembling significantly boosts generalization and stability.

In contrast, our \texttt{deberta-v3-large} (435M parameters) is significantly more expensive. Due to the 2-hour constraint, we were limited to a \textbf{single best model}. While our single model demonstrates superior quality (evidenced by higher CV), it lacks the variance-reduction benefits of an ensemble.

\subsection{Limitations}
Our approach has several limitations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ensemble Constraint:} The 2-hour inference limit prevented us from ensembling multiple checkpoints, which likely caused the gap between CV and LB scores.
    \item \textbf{Single Baseline Comparison:} We only compared against one baseline (23rd place). Comparison with top-10 solutions would provide more comprehensive evaluation.
    \item \textbf{No Post-Processing:} Unlike the baseline's LightGBM stacking, we did not employ post-processing techniques.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

We demonstrated that modernizing the backbone to DeBERTa-v3 and aligning training objectives with the ranking nature of the task are key to improving performance on the Google QUEST Q\&A Labeling challenge. Our key findings are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Ranking-aware losses matter:} Replacing BCE with a Ranking + Soft Spearman loss significantly boosts Spearman correlation.
    \item \textbf{Context length is critical:} Extending from 512 to 1024 tokens prevents information loss in long Q\&A pairs.
    \item \textbf{Architecture alone is insufficient:} ``The Dip'' shows that superior architectures cannot compensate for pooling mismatch and context truncation.
\end{enumerate}

Our single-model CV score (0.4228) surpasses the baseline (0.4195), though our Private LB score (0.4066) is lower than the baseline's (0.4123) due to ensemble constraints. Future work could explore model distillation or quantization to enable efficient ensembling within the 2-hour inference limit.
